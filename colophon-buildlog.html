( How this was made · 02 — The build log )

I gave myself
the brief

Most portfolios talk about other people's products. I wanted mine to be the product, and to build it the way I would build anything I designed.

A story in five chapters · roughly a month of evenings
Chapter 01 — The premise

A claim I had to back up

Every designer says they care about craft. I wanted to stop saying it and show it. So I set a rule for myself before I drew a single frame: this site has to be evidence, not a slideshow. If I claim I design and build, the site itself should be the proof.

That one decision shaped everything. It meant no template, no page builder, no borrowed components. It meant writing the HTML, the CSS, and the JavaScript by hand, the same way I would hand work to an engineer, except this time I was the engineer too.

If the work is about craft, the thing presenting it cannot be careless.

Chapter 02 — Finding the voice

Newspaper, not dashboard

The first drafts looked like every other portfolio: a clean grid, a big sans-serif, a lot of white space. It was fine. Fine was the problem. So I tore it down and went somewhere with a point of view: an editorial system, set like a newspaper, with a masthead, a serif that felt printed, and Roman numerals on everything.

Chapter 03 — Building in the open

Designing by shipping

Here is the part most case studies leave out: I did not design this and then build it. I designed it by building it. Ideas only became real once they were on the screen and I could feel them. A lot of them did not survive contact.

I gave the site a panel of live controls, so instead of guessing between two directions I could ship both and toggle. Row hovers, nav layouts, cursors, fonts, the status indicator. Every one of those started as an argument with myself that I settled in pixels instead of in my head.

A decision I am glad I made

Two accents, and only two

It would have been easy to let the site get colourful. I held it to one amber and one blue, with a single fixed green for the "open to work" status. Restraint reads as confidence. The work brings the colour; the chrome stays quiet.

Chapter 04 — What broke

The bugs that taught me something

Building it myself meant I owned every problem, including the ones I caused. A few were worth the scars.

The disappearing content

Animations that strand

Entrance animations started from opacity: 0 and faded in. Elegant, until a backgrounded tab froze the animation clock and left whole sections invisible. The fix became a principle: the base state is the visible state, and you animate from hidden, never to visible. Now a stalled clock, a print, or a reduced-motion setting always shows content.

The cramped guestbook

A form that fit the desk but not the phone

The guestbook lets visitors press a personal seal onto a wall. The input pill floated neatly over the wall on a laptop and collapsed into an overlapping mess on a phone. The lesson was an old one, relearned by hand: a thing positioned absolutely inside a fixed box has nowhere to go when the box gets small. It had to drop out and stack.

None of these were glamorous. All of them are the difference between a site that looks built and one that is built.

Chapter 05 — Restraint

Knowing when to stop

The hardest part was not adding things. Once you can build anything, every idea looks shippable. I prototyped a card layout, a film-strip of screens, a wax seal, draggable sticky notes. Most of them were good. Most of them did not make it.

The guestbook stayed because it was personal and quiet. The rest got tabled, because a portfolio that shouts is just noise. One thousand no's for every yes. The discipline of cutting your own good ideas is, it turns out, most of the job.

How it actually went
Week 01

Strip it back

Threw out the clean-grid draft. Committed to the editorial direction, the masthead, and the two-typeface system.

Week 02

The work index

Built the case studies and the home index. Argued with myself about rows versus cards, and gave both to a tweak panel.

Week 03

Personality

The cursor, the retro mode, the recruiter view, the multilingual intro. The parts that make it feel like a place, not a page.

Week 04

Polish and cut

The guestbook, motion fixes, mobile passes, and the long list of good ideas I decided not to ship.

( What I would do differently )

It is never finished,
only shipped

If I started again, I would write the design system first instead of discovering it halfway through, and I would reach for real components sooner instead of hand-rolling each one. I would also resist building a feature just because I could.

But I am glad it grew the way it did, by hand, in the open, one stubborn decision at a time. The site you are reading this on is not a description of how I work. It is the work.
